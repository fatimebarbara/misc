Four versions of one email

The prompt ladder

A beneficiary's interim report is late. The facts never change — only your prompt does. Each version adds one block and fixes something, and the full prompt is repeated every time so you can copy it straight out. Problems fall 4 → 3 → 1 → 0.

The five blocks

In the order that matters — not the order you'd write them in.

Task
What you want, and what finished looks like. The goal is what gives the answer an ending.
Context
Who's reading, the facts, and what must not be said. Negative instructions are the most underused line in any prompt.
Context includes what you attach. Attach only material you are authorised to upload to the tool you're using. Watch for personal data, confidential business information, evaluation material, or anything with a sensitivity marking. When in doubt, ask before you upload.
Rules
What to do when it doesn't know — flag it, ask you, or leave it blank. The block almost nobody writes.
Output
Format, length, tone, examples. A word limit edits better than "be concise".
Role Optional
"You are an experienced project officer." The move you reach for first, and the one that changes least.

In its favour
  • It was fast, and it is shaped like an email.
Four problems
  • 1 · It states something false. Something arrived on 14 August. The first real line tells her it didn't count.
  • 2 · It invents authority. You gave it no grant agreement and authorised no consequence.
  • 3 · Eleven placeholders. It gives you a shape and hands the work back.
  • 4 · No usable ask, wrong register. To someone with a clean record, this reads as a first warning.
Biggest problem
It invented a rule and a threat, then wrote them calmly.
The placeholders you'd notice. The two sentences about the Grant Agreement you might not — they sound exactly like sentences that belong in this email.

Added by me, not by you

  • Friday 4 September — invented. Roughly three weeks out; I don't know your payment cycle, your leave, or hers.
  • "A short note" on the underspend — my judgement of what's proportionate. If a 40% deviation needs formal justification under your rules, harden it.
  • "Twenty minutes, any day next week" — invented. Change if your week is full.
  • Left out on purpose: any link between the complete package and the next payment. You didn't give me your payment terms and I won't imply a consequence you haven't authorised.
  • Worth knowing: staff who left in June wouldn't have been charged to the project afterwards — that may well be the underspend. A lead for you, not a line for the email.

If you remember nothing else

Five blocks is one too many for a busy Tuesday. Use three.

Task
What you want, and what done looks like.
Context
Who's reading, the facts, what not to say — and attach what you're allowed to attach.
Output
Format, length, tone.
  • Then the upgrade: add Rules — mark what you added, flag what's missing or unclear, ask me before guessing.
  • Always read the draft. AI writes confidently even when it's wrong — you are the editor.
  • Check the facts yourself — dates, deadlines, figures, names, references.

Most people stop at version three, because version three looks finished.

Try it once on a real email this week.
