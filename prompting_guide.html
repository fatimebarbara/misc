Prompting · four versions of one email

The prompt ladder

You have a beneficiary whose interim report is late. Below, the same situation is prompted four times — the facts never change, only what you write. Each version adds one block, fixes something specific, and breaks nothing. Watch the problem count fall: 4 → 3 → 1 → 0. Anything in yellow is yours to change.

The five blocks

In the order they actually matter — which is not the order you probably write them in.

1
Task

What you want, and what “finished” looks like. Not “write an email” but what has to be true afterwards. The goal is the part you’ll be tempted to leave out, and it’s the part that gives the answer an ending.

2
Context

Who is reading, what the facts are, what the constraints are — and what must not be said. Negative instructions are the most underused line in any prompt. Give a sensitive fact and its handling rule in the same breath.

Context includes what you attach

Your fastest upgrade is usually to stop describing a document and attach it — the call text, the grant agreement, the report you received, the notes you took.

Attach only material you are authorised to upload to the tool you are using. Before you attach anything, check that the tool is approved for that category of information, and consider whether the document contains: personal data; commercially sensitive or confidential business information belonging to applicants or beneficiaries; proposals or evaluation material under assessment; or anything carrying a sensitivity or classification marking. If you need a document that can’t go up as-is, extract or redact the part you actually need.

Check this against the real rules. The categories above are the shape of the question, not the answer. What binds you are [your organisation’s] rules on data protection, information handling, and which AI tools are approved for which content. When in doubt, ask before you upload.

3
Rules

What it may assert, and what it should do when it doesn’t know. Verify before writing. Leave a marked gap rather than filling it. Flag inferences as inferences. Put consequences in a note to me, not in the deliverable. This is the block almost nobody writes — and the only one that changes whether you can trust what comes back rather than just enjoy reading it.

4
Output

Format, length, tone — and examples, which belong here rather than in context, because an example mostly teaches shape. A word limit will do more editing for you than any instruction to “be concise”.

5
Role Optional · least of these

“You are an experienced project officer.” It helps set vocabulary and assumed background, and it’s the move you’ll reach for first — which is why it sits last here. Below, it changes less than a single line about tone. Useful, oversold.

Rules The block that matters
Before writing, run this and show it to me: ▪ Which facts came from me, and which are you inferring? ▪ Are you about to assert a consequence I didn't give you? ▪ What are you guessing at? Don't cite any article, clause or procedural consequence unless I gave it to you. If you think one belongs, put it in a note below the email, not in the email. Leave a marked gap rather than filling it. Never state an inference as established fact.

This is what changed · your Rules block produced it

Checks I ran, and what I left out

  • From you vs. inferred. Everything in the email came from your brief. The one inference is that “a short note” is a proportionate ask for the underspend — see below.
  • A consequence I did not assert: payment. There is very likely a link between the complete package and the next payment, but you didn’t give me your payment terms, so I won’t imply a financial consequence you didn’t authorise. Add a plain sentence yourself if you want it in.
  • I guessed the date. 4 September is roughly three weeks out — a guess, not a judgement. I don’t know your payment cycle, your leave, or hers.
  • I may be under-formalising the underspend. If a 40% deviation triggers a formal justification requirement, “a short note” is too casual. Attach the grant agreement and I can check the reporting terms instead of leaving this blank.
  • The restructuring, left out as instructed — but worth knowing: staff who left in June wouldn’t have been charged to the project afterwards, so it may well be the underspend. A lead for you, not a line for the email.
Fixed
  • 7 · You can’t tell what it guessed — now you can. Every guess, every omission, every thing it declined to claim, listed for you.
  • The email barely changed. That’s the part worth noticing: this version didn’t write better, it told you what to check before you press send.
  • The last note came out of a fact you forbade. It couldn’t use the restructuring in the email, so it handed it back to you as a lead on the underspend.
  • Role did almost nothing — compare the two emails. The tone line you wrote earlier did the work you’d have credited to the persona.
What no prompt fixes for you
  • Nothing here was verified. No system checked, no document opened. Every fact came from you — which is exactly what attaching the grant agreement would change.
  • You still have to read it. The writing is now confident enough that you might stop questioning it. Fluency rises faster than accuracy.

One thing worth knowing

This ladder was built to climb cleanly. Your own prompting won’t always be so tidy — adding context can introduce new problems as well as fix old ones.

Two turned up while this was being written. Handing over the restructuring detail without a handling rule produced an email that put hearsay in writing. And stating the goal — “get the package quickly” — with no Rules block produced an invented sentence about payment being processed: a lever nobody had authorised.

Both are avoided above by pairing each addition with its safeguard. Send a sensitive fact with its constraint, and a goal with its rules. That’s the actual craft.

If you remember nothing else

Five blocks is one too many to hold in your head on a busy Tuesday. Use three.

Task
What you want, and what done looks like.
Context
Who’s reading, the facts, what not to say — and attach what you’re allowed to attach.
Output
Format, length, tone.
  • Then the upgrade: add Rules — verify first, flag what you’re guessing, leave gaps marked rather than filled.
  • Always read the draft. AI writes confidently even when it’s wrong — you are the editor.
  • Check the facts yourself — dates, deadlines, figures, names and references.
  • Fill in every [placeholder] before sending.

Most people stop at version three, because version three looks finished.

Try it once on a real email this week. Adjust anything to fit how you work.
