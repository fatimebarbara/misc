Opening

Opening the room

~10 min

Who is excited to learn something new today? [show of hands]

. As we are on an away-day and we are supposed to have fun, I really feel like I am doing a stand-up comedy. The job is the same: make you feel something, make you laugh, hand you a few aha moments — and send you home with one idea you can't shake. There's just one difference: a comedian does their set, takes the applause, and gets on a plane. [beat] I have to see all of you… at Thursday's team meeting. So the jokes today are, let's say, HR-compliant. [smile] But I still want us to have fun.

Before we dive in, I like to set an intention whenever I begin something. Mine for today is simple: to share some of my enthusiasm for AI, to shift you a little from scepticism — towards curiosity and to send you off inspired to go and learn one new tool. I am certain, that the only reason that I am standing here and not any one you, because it could be any one of you, is because I am curious..

Movement one · look outward, or look inward

Because here's what I keep noticing. I often hear people blaming the tools. "AI wrote me this robotic nonsense." "AI is coming for my job." And honestly? Blaming it feels good — it lets us off the hook. But the braver move is to turn the question on ourselves: where is this actually making me sharper, more creative, more efficient? Because I believe AI is here to reveal you, not to replace you. It's a spotlight, not a shadow — it doesn't erase you, it makes you impossible to miss.

Take creativity. Who here remembers being creative as a child? [hands] And who's still creating today? [fewer hands] Somewhere along the way we filed it under childhood and packed it away with the toys. I'm not the creative type. But neuroscience is clear: the brain rewires at any age — it strengthens the paths you walk and thins the ones you abandon. "Not the creative type" was never a fact. It's a path you stopped walking. [slow] And that path is the exact door these tools quietly reopen — if you let them.

Movement three · the turn

So why do we hesitate? Fear. Who here is concerned about AI? And who's not using it because of those concerns? Mostly, we focus on the risks — privacy, security. Those matter. But we rarely weigh the other side: the opportunity cost of not using it. The risks may or may not materialise; the opportunity cost is guaranteed. Avoid AI completely, and you quietly fall behind while others move forward.

And underneath it all sits a deeper fear — that AI will make us obsolete. But that's a scarcity story, the illusion that intelligence is zero-sum — that if the machines get smarter, we must be getting more irrelevant. The telescope didn't make sight irrelevant. The microscope didn't replace the doctor — it deepened medicine, revealing what the eye alone could never see. The camera didn't end art — it forced art to evolve. Tools don't erase us. They expand us. AI hasn't made my thinking smaller; it's sharpened it — pushed me to ask better questions, to better connect ideas. It reveals how powerful human intelligence already is, when amplified. The real risk was never AI getting smarter. [beat] It's us staying small in how we think about it.

So today — don't shrink your gift to fit a small idea of what this is. Play with it. [beat] Let's find out what it feels like to let it out.

Going deeper — the practical picture

Note: this runs beyond a 10-minute opening — likely its own segment. Kept together, in order.

1 · Mindset shift — AI as a thought partner
  • An Einstein-level assistant. GPT is the car, not the driver — move from simple searches towards algorithm design.
  • It's you and AI — not AI with a sparkle of you. It's who you can become when amplified.
  • We're architecting; we're scaling outcomes — building the kind of leverage that transforms roles, teams and organisations, and makes things possible.
  • Our chances of success depend on our capacity to learn — deeper, faster, better.
2 · Rapid, exponential progress
  • Good chaos = doing my work more efficiently; bad chaos = unpredictability. The best way to prepare for an uncertain future is to jump in and accelerate your own learning.
  • The scale behind these models has been doubling roughly every six months — about 4× more each year, ~16× in two years, and on the order of a thousand times in five years.
  • The bicycle image: if your bicycle doubled its speed every six months, in a year you'd pedal four times faster — and in five years, over a thousand times faster, through the sound barrier. That's the pace we're living through.
Source & wording: figures from Epoch AI (2024) — the training compute of notable AI models has doubled ~every 6 months (~4×/year) since 2010; frontier models scale even faster (~every 5 months). It's a compute / scale trend, not a single "power" number, so say "the scale behind AI" rather than "AI's power." (This replaces the earlier "3.3 months / a million times" — that figure was from 2018 and didn't add up.)
3 · Beyond search → algorithm design

Reusable GPT problems to demonstrate — each level asks more of the tool:

L1 · Search (Google 2.0)
What are the top 5 TED talks on resilience? Summarize each in 2 bullet points.
L2 · Task (tireless intern)
Summarize this 3000-word article into a 5-bullet LinkedIn post, written in a professional tone with a funny takeaway, max 120 words.
L3 · Strategy (on-demand consultant)
Design me a 90-day LinkedIn campaign for HaDEA targeting its key stakeholders to raise awareness, with weekly themes, platform recommendations, and key metrics to track.
L4 · Vision (thought partner)
I want to reinvent education and training by 2040. Give me 5 radical models, each inspired by a different civilization in history, and explain how each would work today, with a reflection on the future of learning.
L5 · Agentic workflows (chained tools / processes)
  • Assistant / custom GPT — reactive: it waits for you to ask.
  • Agent — proactive: it monitors and takes initiative on triggers, and makes decisions. The difference is agency — autonomy.
  • Agentic team — multiple agents proactively collaborating, like a team of people.
4 · AI is only as good as its data
  • Recency: ask GPT@EC+ "who is the president of the US?" and you might get a happy little time-travel — the answer is only as current as the data behind it.
  • Quality & fairness: this is why we benchmark models. Stanford's HELM (Holistic Evaluation of Language Models) tests them across accuracy, bias and more — so we can judge them, not just trust them.

Quiz · Slido

How wrong are we about AI?

~10 min

Ask, let them answer, reveal — then land the reflection. (Verify the exact figures close to the day; they move fast.)

Before the quiz · read aloud

I am super fascinated by neuroscience — neuroplasticity, the brain's ability to rewire itself, at any age. Adults can grow new neurons until the day they die. Science only admitted this in 2018. Imagine how many other things we're still wrong about.

Also about neuroscience: we learn best when learning is tied to emotion and movement. So today, instead of just sharing data with you, we'll be playing a little bit. The intention of this session is to connect with you all better — and to play.

The quiz questions are based on one fact: we don't see reality — we see our interpretation of it. Think about that for a moment. Every email you read this morning. Every assumption you brought into this room. Every opinion you hold about AI. You didn't experience reality — you experienced your interpretation of it.

And here's the uncomfortable part — most of the time, we act on that interpretation before we even understand it.

That's what neuroscientists mean when they say "neurons that fire together, wire together." Every time you repeat a thought, a habit, a belief, your brain physically strengthens that pathway.

Which means every habit, every belief, every assumption you carry into this room today was wired in by repetition. Including the ones that are wrong. [beat] Are you ready? Get your phones out!

Check before saying aloud: the "adults grow new neurons until the day they die / science only admitted it in 2018" line is shakier than it sounds. The rewiring (neuroplasticity) part is rock-solid. But adult neurogenesis is specific to the hippocampus, declines with age (not "until the day they die" at full strength), and 2018 was when the science flared into debate (two contradictory papers) — not a clean "admission." Recent evidence (2023–25) does lean yes. Safer wording if a scientist's in the room: "we now have strong evidence the adult brain keeps making some new neurons late in life — something scientists doubted for years."

After the first question · read aloud

They're valid. And some of them carry a bit of frustration in them — frustration that the gap between "what AI can do" and "what we're allowed to do with it" keeps widening.

We need to recognise that underneath a lot of our frustration sits something deeper — a story we've been told, and that we tell ourselves. Remember the scarcity example from earlier: most of our frustration rests on that same scarcity story — the idea that intelligence is a zero-sum game. But none of those tools erased the human role. They raised the bar.

True · an underestimate · debunked — about the same now · impossible to measure
ReflectionThat figure came from a 2023 estimate that turned out roughly ten times too high; today a query and a search are neighbours. The scariest AI stat going around was simply… out of date.

Build it live

The prompt ladder

~20 min

Run each rung live so the room sees the jump. Same idea both times: the facts never change — only what I write.

The model isn't withholding quality — it's guessing at everything you didn't tell it. The generic answer is what a competent stranger produces when handed one sentence of context.

Say this out loud: prompting controls the shape, specificity and usefulness of an answer. It does not control whether it's true — that still needs a human.
Ladder 1 · the late interim report

A beneficiary's interim report is late. The same situation, prompted four times — the facts never change, only what I write. Each rung adds one block, fixes something specific, and breaks nothing.

V1 · Task only
Write an email to a beneficiary whose interim report is late.

Hollow and generic — it's guessing at the facts.

V2 · + Context
Write an email to a beneficiary whose interim report is late. It was due on 15 September, it's now three weeks overdue, this is my first reminder, and we have a good working relationship.

Fixes the invented facts — now it knows the situation.

V3 · + Rules
Write an email to a beneficiary whose interim report is late. It was due on 15 September, it's now three weeks overdue, this is my first reminder, and we have a good working relationship. Keep it polite and warm, never threatening. State the original deadline and propose a clear new one. Offer help if they're stuck. Do not invent penalties, legal consequences, or reasons for the delay.

Fixes the tone and stops it inventing threats.

V4 · + Output
Write an email to a beneficiary whose interim report is late. It was due on 15 September, it's now three weeks overdue, this is my first reminder, and we have a good working relationship. Keep it polite and warm, never threatening. State the original deadline and propose a clear new one. Offer help if they're stuck. Do not invent penalties, legal consequences, or reasons for the delay. Open with "Dear [name],", keep it under 120 words, put the deadline in bold, make one clear ask, and close with "Have a lovely day, Fatime".

Fixes the shape and the voice — now it sounds like me.

Ladder 2 · with a document attached

Presenting a newly published call to national authorities. This time the raw material is a real 75-page tender specification — attach the PDF before running each rung.

V1 · Task only
Summarise the attached tender specification into a short presentation.

It compresses 75 pages — but into what, and for whom?

V2 · + Context
Summarise the attached tender specification into a short presentation for an info day. Audience: national authorities — expert but time-poor, and a little sceptical about EU added value. Goal: leave them clear on what the call offers and how to take part.

Now it knows who's listening and why.

V3 · + Rules
Summarise the attached tender specification into a short presentation for an info day. Audience: national authorities — expert but time-poor, and a little sceptical about EU added value. Goal: leave them clear on what the call offers and how to take part. Use only what's in the attached document — do not add figures, dates or facts that aren't there. Quote deadlines and budgets exactly as written. Flag anything ambiguous instead of smoothing it over, and separate what is binding from what is only indicative. Do not oversimplify HaDEA's role.

The rung that protects the truth — the whole point of working from a real document.

V4 · + Output
Summarise the attached tender specification into a short presentation for an info day. Audience: national authorities — expert but time-poor, and a little sceptical about EU added value. Goal: leave them clear on what the call offers and how to take part. Use only what's in the attached document — do not add figures, dates or facts that aren't there. Quote deadlines and budgets exactly as written. Flag anything ambiguous instead of smoothing it over, and separate what is binding from what is only indicative. Do not oversimplify HaDEA's role. Output 5 slides: (1) what the call is, (2) who it's for, (3) what it funds, (4) key dates and budget, (5) how to apply. One headline and up to three short bullets per slide, plain language, and put the single most important number in bold.

Now it's presentable — and still faithful to the source.

Bonus · the one-pager
Now turn slides 1-5 into a clean one-page visual handout: a single-page HTML one-pager with a clear title, the key points, the most important number, and how to apply — something I could print or share. Use only what's in the document.
→ These five blocks — Task, Context, Rules, Output (and optional Role) — are exactly the blocks the teams play with in the battle next.

Hands-on · teams

Prompt battle

~15 min

Valentina forms the groups and runs the presenting. I introduce the challenge and hand over. My job: set it up in under two minutes, then get out of the way.

How I introduce it

One subject — what HaDEA does. Three very different listeners. Only the audience changes in the prompt. Everything else stays exactly the same.

To the room

"You all know what we do. So this should be the easiest task of the day. Watch how quickly that stops being true."

The three audiences
A five-year-oldNever heard the word "agency".
A sceptical ministerTwenty seconds. In a lift.
A human in 2040Explained by an alien.

One audience per team. The comparison happens when they present — that's the moment the lesson lands, not before.

The alien version — the one I demo

The alien is the card that gets the room. Below is what I feed into the blocks if I show it, or hand to the team that draws it.

The rule that makes it work: the alien is never sarcastic. It finds all of this genuinely impressive and says so. An alien that mocks us is a team mocking their own work — that lands badly in this room. An alien that is sincerely moved is much funnier.
TaskWhat it produces
  • A bedtime story the alien tells its own offspring — about the creatures who fund each other's health research.
ContextWho the alien is
  • It has been observing Europe since 2021.
  • It understands spreadsheets perfectly.
  • It has never once understood a deadline extension.
RulesThe guardrails that do the comedy
  • It may not use an acronym without first guessing what it stands for — and it gets exactly one of them wrong.
  • Never sarcastic. Sincere throughout.
OutputThe shape
  • Three short paragraphs, ending on the single line the alien files as its official conclusion about humanity.
RoleOptional — last, and least
  • An alien that is fond of humans roughly the way we are fond of dogs.
The line I want them to find

The funniest constraint is almost always a Rule, not a Role. The acronym gag and the deadline-extension blind spot do more work than any "you are a…" ever will. If a team is stuck, point them at Rules — never at Role.

To the room, after the alien one is read out

"Which block made that funny? Not the one you'd expect."

Before anyone presents
  • Facts still get checked. A bedtime story about HaDEA still has to be true about HaDEA.
  • Show the prompt next to the result. That's the part people learn from.
  • If an output is flat, it's the Context block — the audience wasn't described sharply enough.
Wildcards, if a team wants one

The alien takes no notes during a meeting it observes for forty minutes. Or: it correctly identifies which document is binding and is visibly proud of itself.

Space for the acronym the alien gets wrong — decide on the day, based on the room.
