Four versions of one presentation

The prompt ladder
with a document attached

You have to present a newly published call to national authorities. The source is a real 75-page tender specification — which moves the value somewhere different. The full prompt is repeated every time so you can copy it out. Problems fall 7 → 4 → 1 → 0.

The five blocks

In the order that matters — not the order you'd write them in.

Task
What you want, and what finished looks like. The goal is what gives the answer an ending.
Context
Who's reading, the facts, and what to leave out. Negative instructions are the most underused line in any prompt.
Context includes what you attach. Attach only material you are authorised to upload to the tool you're using. Watch for personal data, confidential business information, evaluation material, or anything with a sensitivity marking. When in doubt, ask before you upload.
Rules
What to do when it doesn't know — flag it, ask you, or leave it blank. The block almost nobody writes.
Output
Format, length, tone, examples. A word or slide limit edits better than "be concise".
Role Optional
"You are an experienced project officer." The move you reach for first, and the one that changes least.

In its favour
  • The objectives on slide 3 happen to be close to the real ones. The rest is decoration.
Seven problems
  • 1 · Figures with no source. €5 million, 30 workshops, 1,000 officials — all invented, all wrong.
  • 2 · Nothing specific to this call. Your audience already knows what BTSF is.
  • 3 · Wrong about the process. Participants are nominated through NCPs, not self-selected. Someone will correct this out loud.
  • 4 · Pitched at nobody. Could be for bidders, officials or the public.
  • 5 · No goal, no ask. "Contact your NCP" — half the room is the NCP.
  • 6 · Ignores the scepticism. Nothing on why this is worth their staff time.
  • 7 · You can't tell what came from where. Because nothing did.
Biggest problem
It made up three numbers and said them with a straight face.
Everything else you could fix by editing. Invented figures are only caught if someone in the room knows better — and if nobody does, they end up in the minutes.

What is firm, what is not, and what isn't in the document at all

  • Firm requirements. Workshop counts (19 + 6), the days (4 and 3), 650 trained participant units per period, minimum tutor numbers, the €3,900,000 ceiling, the 60-day invitation notice, the reporting months.
  • Examples, not commitments. The subjects on slide 3. The document presents these as illustrations of scope for tenderers, expressly not the final deliverables. Present them as the programme and you will be quoted on it.
  • A forecast, not an allocation. The participant split of roughly 89% Member States, 8% Candidate Countries, 3% EFTA and others is given to help tenderers plan financially, and quotas may be adjusted.
  • Conditional. The second 30-month period depends on budget, on need continuing, and on approval of the final report. "60 months" flat would overstate it.
  • Missing — and they will ask. The submission deadline is not in these specifications; the document points to the contract notice and the Portal. I haven't guessed a date.
  • Missing. Dates, locations and the contractor. The timeline runs in contract months from signature, not calendar dates.
  • Added by me, not the document. "Put your inspector in a room with counterparts from across the EU" on slide 4 — that's an argument I constructed, not a statement from the source. Yours to stand behind.
Fixed
  • 7 · You can't tell what came from where — every slide sorted into firm, example, forecast or conditional.
  • Look at what the edits are. "Conditional on", "not yet fixed", "a planning estimate", "not known yet". Every one puts back a hedge version 3 had dropped.
  • The most valuable line is about an absence. The deadline isn't in the specifications at all — version 3 would have carried an invented one onto a slide.
What no prompt fixes
  • It read one document. The contract notice and the Portal page are separate — and one of them holds the deadline.
  • Slide 4 is an argument, not a fact. Whether it lands is your judgement.
  • You still have to read it. A deck sourced entirely from a real document is the most convincing wrong deck you'll ever produce.

If you remember nothing else

When there is a document, three lines get you most of the way.

Attach it
Subject to your organisation's rules on what may be uploaded.
Say who it's for
And what to leave out. The negative list cuts more than the positive one adds.
Ask what's firm
Requirement, example, forecast or condition — make it sort them before you present.
  • "Use only the attached document." One sentence that stops it topping up from memory.
  • Ask what's missing, not just what's there. The deadline wasn't in the file.
  • Check the figures against the source before they go on a slide with your name on it.

A deck built entirely from a real document is the most convincing wrong deck you will ever produce.
