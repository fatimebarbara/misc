Seven problems

What is firm, what is not, and what isn’t in here at all

  • Firm. The workshop counts (19 + 6), the days (4 and 3), the 650 trained participant units per period, the minimum tutor numbers, the €3,900,000 ceiling, the 60-day invitation notice, the reporting months and payment percentages. These are contractual requirements.
  • Not firm — examples. The thematic areas on slide 3. The document states these are examples of thematic areas to help tenderers prepare, and expressly not the final deliverables. If you present them as the programme, you will be quoted on it.
  • Not firm — a forecast. The participant split of roughly 89% EU Member States, 8% Candidate Countries, 3% EFTA / Northern Ireland / Neighbourhood is given to help tenderers make a financial forecast, and the contracting authority reserves the right to adjust country quotas. It is not an allocation you can promise anyone.
  • Not firm — conditional. The second 30-month period depends on budget availability, on training needs persisting, and on approval of the final report. Presenting “60 months” flat would overstate it.
  • Missing, and they will ask: the deadline. The submission deadline is not in these specifications — the document refers to the contract notice and the Funding & Tenders Portal. I have not guessed at a date. Get it from the notice.
  • Missing: dates, locations, contractor. The timeline runs in contract months from signature (M0, M3, M5, M18, M29–30), not calendar dates, and no contractor is selected. Nobody can be told when or where the first workshop will be.
  • Where I stayed vaguer than you might expect. Slide 7 says certificates depend on completing the pre- and post-course activities, without pinning down the exact trigger. The document sets out the conditions across more than one section, so quote the passage you can point to rather than a paraphrase of both.
  • One thing I deliberately did not add. Nothing about how this compares to the previous BTSF plant health contract. I have no document for that, and general recollection is exactly what you told me not to use.

Why this one is different from the email ladder

With an email, the facts live in your head and the prompt’s job is to get them out. With a published document the facts are already written down — so the jump from version 1 to version 2 is the biggest on this ladder, and it costs one paperclip.

But an attachment brings a risk no email has. A tender specification contains sentences that carry very different weight, and they all look the same on the page. Same font, same paragraph, no boxes, no “indicative only” banner. The only thing marking a sentence as soft is a word or two buried inside it:

Binding
“The organisation of nineteen (19) workshops of four (4) full days training each.” No hedge. The contractor must deliver this.
Illustrative
The thematic subjects. The document says these are examples of thematic areas to help tenderers prepare, and expressly not the final deliverables.
A forecast
The participant split of roughly 89 / 8 / 3. It is roughly envisaged, given so bidders can build a financial forecast, and the quotas can be adjusted.
Conditional
The second 30-month period. Subject to budget availability, the need continuing, and approval of the final report.

Now the failure. Compressing seventy-five pages into four bullets means the qualifier is the first thing to go — bullets are short, hedges are long, and “roughly envisaged” looks like padding rather than information. So “roughly envisaged, around 89%” becomes “89% from Member States”, and an estimate has quietly turned into an allocation someone will hold you to. “Subject to” becomes “60 months”. “Examples” becomes a programme.

Nothing was invented. Every figure traces back to the document, so checking it confirms it — and the thing that went missing was never in the number. It was in the word next to it.

That is what the Rules block is for here. Not accuracy in the ordinary sense — version 3 was already accurate — but knowing which accurate things you are allowed to promise.

If you remember nothing else

When there is a document, three lines get you most of the way.

Attach it
Don’t describe a document you could upload — subject to your organisation’s rules on what may be uploaded.
Say who it’s for
And say what to leave out. The negative list cuts more than the positive one adds.
Ask what’s firm
Requirement, example, forecast or condition — make it sort them, and flag where you’d be more precise than the source.
  • “Use only the attached document.” One sentence that stops it topping up from memory.
  • Ask what’s missing, not just what’s there. The deadline wasn’t in the file.
  • Check the figures against the source before they go on a slide with your name on it.

A deck built entirely from a real document is the most convincing wrong deck you will ever produce.
